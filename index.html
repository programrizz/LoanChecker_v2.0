Of the applicants who went on to repay, the model approved 28% of under 25s and 48% of everyone older.

LoanChecker is an explainable credit risk model with a fairness audit built in. Using 1,000 historical loans from the UCI German Credit dataset, it traces where that age gap comes from and tests whether it can be closed without making lending more expensive.

The question

Can a credit risk model accurately pick out the applicants likely to repay while systematically disadvantaging younger ones, and if it does, can that be fixed without raising the lender's costs?

The project breaks that into five research questions:

  1. How effectively can machine learning models distinguish between applicants who repay and those who default?
  2. Do applicants under 25 experience systematically lower approval rates?
  3. Does removing age from the model eliminate the observed disparity?
  4. Can alternative decision thresholds improve fairness while maintaining comparable lending costs?
  5. What are the limitations and potential consequences of these interventions?

This is a research prototype. It is not a production credit scoring system, and nothing here is a recommendation for real lending policy.

Method

One small, well studied dataset, three models, a cost rule from the dataset's own documentation, and a fairness audit run on predictions the models never trained on.

The data is the Statlog German Credit dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository: loans made by a bank in southern Germany between 1973 and 1975, each labelled as a good risk (repaid) or a bad risk. The original codebook mislabels several categories, so every label was checked against the corrected South German Credit codebook (Grömping, 2019) before any modelling.

Why the label check matters. With the old labels, "no checking account" looked like the strongest sign of a good borrower. That code really means "200 DM or more, or a salary account". The numbers don't change, but explanations built on the old labels would be wrong.

The 5:1 cost rule

The dataset's documentation says approving a bad risk costs 5 times as much as rejecting a good one. If the model gives an applicant a probability p of repaying, lend only when the expected cost of lending is lower:

5(1 − p) < p  so  p > 56

The model must be about 83.3% sure before it lends.

This threshold reflects the assumed relative costs. It is not a universal lending threshold.

Why this one number matters for fairness. A strict line turns away many borderline applicants, and young applicants are more likely to be borderline.

How the audit runs

  1. Translate and check the dataCodes such as A11 become plain English, matched to the corrected codebook.
  2. Train three models with nested cross validation5 folds: train on 800 applicants, predict the hidden 200, repeat until everyone has been predicted once while unseen. An inner loop tunes each model's regularisation.
  3. Apply the 5/6 cut-off and compare costsAgainst two baselines: approve everyone and reject everyone.
  4. Measure approvals by ageUnder 25 against 25 or older, with 95% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples.
  5. Trace the gap and test proxiesSplit the score gap detail by detail, then check whether other details give age away.
  6. Test fixes and their costDelete age, delete age and its proxies, reweigh the training data, or use a separate threshold for under 25s.
  7. Stress the resultA flip test, calibration by age, a like for like comparison, a cliff edge check at 25, and a recession simulation.

The main fairness measure is the approval rate among good risks: of the people who really did repay, what share got a loan? It asks exactly whether creditworthy under 25s are penalised, and it doesn't punish a model for real differences in repayment.

Its weakness is that it trusts the good and bad labels.

Accuracy and cost pull in opposite directions

At a 50% threshold the models beat approve everyone on accuracy, about 75% against 70%, but they cost far more than rejecting everyone. At the 5/6 cut-off the cost drops well below 700, while accuracy falls under 70%, because the model now turns away many good applicants to avoid the expensive bad ones. Which is better depends on what the bank cares about, so both are reported.

Against the reject everyone baseline of 700, logistic regression saves 85 to 215 cost points (95% interval). It was also the least overfitted: random forest scored 96% on the data it trained on and 76% on data it hadn't seen.

Cost is scaled to 1,000 applicants: 5 points for each bad loan approved and 1 point for each good applicant rejected.

The more flexible models were also less fair: their age gaps among good risks were 26.9 and 31.8 points.

Key fairness findings

Under 25s did repay less often than older applicants. The audit asks whether the model's gap is bigger than that real difference, and why.

The gap is bigger than the real difference in repayment

59.1% of under 25s repaid, against 71.9% of applicants 25 or older: a real difference of 12.9 points. The model's approval gap is 19.2 points (95% interval 12.0 to 26.1), and among applicants who repaid it is 19.5 points. Under 25s are approved at half the rate of older applicants, a ratio of 0.50 (0.33 to 0.68).

A ratio below 0.8 fails the four fifths rule, a US rule of thumb from employment law. It is used here as a yardstick, not a UK legal test.

It doesn't depend on choosing 25

The audit was re-run with every age cut-off from under 22 to under 35. The approval gap stayed between 15.6 and 19.5 points throughout, so the finding is not an artefact of where the line was drawn.

Removing age barely helps, because other details give it away

With age deleted from the model, the overall approval gap only falls from 19.2 to 17.9 points. With age hidden, the remaining details still predict whether an applicant is under 25 with an AUC of 0.80, where 0.5 is guessing. The strongest proxies are the family status and sex code (0.68), housing (0.66) and years in the current job (0.63).

This is why simply removing a protected characteristic may not remove a disparity.

Deleting age and its top three proxies cuts the approval gap to 12.0 points, with accuracy slipping from 58.5% to 57.2%.

A handful of details drive the score gap

Because a logistic regression score is a weighted sum, the average score gap between older and younger applicants splits exactly into one piece per detail. The largest pieces are checking account status (0.22 log odds), age itself (0.20), the family status and sex code (0.12), housing (0.11) and credit history (0.09).

A useful test for each driver: does it measure whether someone can repay, or how far along in life they are? Checking account balance and credit history are about repayment. Age, job length and renting or owning mostly measure life stage.

The model doesn't misjudge young people's risk

The model is about equally well calibrated for both groups. Across under 25s it predicted 61.3% would repay, and 59.1% did, a difference of −2.3 points (interval −9.7 to +4.9). The gap comes from where the approval line falls: more young applicants sit just below it. In the band just under the line, 17 of 20 under 25s and 107 of 129 older applicants repaid.

With only 149 young applicants, a mismatch of up to about 10 points could still hide in the noise.

Most of the gap survives a like for like comparison

Comparing applicants with the same checking account status and savings band, and similar loan amounts and lengths, 11.3 points of the 19.2 point gap remain (5.1 to 17.8). Among good risks, 13.6 of the 19.5 points remain (4.7 to 23.3). Roughly 40% of the gap is explained by young applicants' different finances; the rest is not.

Which details count as legitimate is a judgement. The audit used the four most clearly about the loan and the applicant's money, and left out life stage details such as housing and job length.

Changing only the age rarely flips a decision

Changing nothing but an applicant's age, 2 of the 149 under 25s would switch from rejected to approved at 35, and 45 of the 851 older applicants would lose an approval at 22.

A flip test only catches age's direct effect. A model with age deleted would pass it perfectly and still have a large gap, so passing it doesn't prove a model is fair.

At a realistic default rate, the gap shrinks

30% of the loans in the data went bad, but the real share is reported to be about 5%. Correcting the model's odds to a 5% base rate turns the same 5:1 rule into a cut-off of 0.38. The approval ratio rises to 0.91, and the gap among good risks falls to 4.5 points (−0.9 to +11.1). The headline numbers are a result for this oversampled data: a real bank would be less strict, and the gap smaller.

The three in sample separate threshold rows chose their cut-off on the same predictions they are then scored on, so their costs are slightly optimistic. The out of fold version picks the under 25 cut-off without seeing the test fold (0.74 to 0.79 across the five folds), and is the one quoted throughout.

Reweighing gives extra weight during training to under represented combinations, such as young applicants who repaid, so "young means risky" is harder to learn as a shortcut.

Who the fix helps, and who it leapfrogs

The fix approves 18 more under 25s, 15 of whom repaid. But it moves the cliff at 25 rather than removing it. It leaves 122 applicants aged 25 or older rejected with scores that would now get an under 25 approved, and 106 of them (87%) repaid. Good risks aged 25 to 29 end up approved 35% of the time, against 45% for under 25s, the lowest of any band, though that difference is within noise (−10.5 points, interval −23.2 to +3.2).

Lowering the line for everyone instead costs no more (521 against 547, difference −73 to +24) and leapfrogs nobody, but the age gap comes straight back: 19.7 points among good risks, and a ratio of 0.59.

A separate age based threshold raises significant legal and regulatory questions. Deciding differently because of someone's age can itself be unlawful in many countries, and a smaller measured gap doesn't make an intervention lawful or appropriate.

Does fairer lending make the bank riskier in a recession?

A recession simulation over 20,000 possible futures, using a one factor model of shared economic shocks, compared the worst 1% of outcomes for each policy. The separate threshold fix lends to 369 people instead of 351, with the share of lending going to under 25s rising from 6.8% to 10.5%. Its 99% Value at Risk rises from 321,653 DM to 340,935 DM, or from 36.6% to 37.2% of the money lent.

The strength of the shared shock (a correlation of 0.2) is an assumption the dataset can't measure, and default chances come from oversampled data, so absolute losses are too high.

Limitations

The results should be read in the context of the dataset, the modelling assumptions and the experimental design.

LimitationWhy it mattersHow to overcome it
Small data1,000 loans, 149 under 25Wide confidence intervals; results could shift with more dataRepeat the audit on a larger, modern dataset
Old data1973 to 1975, one German bankYoung people's finances and lending rules have changed a lot since thenRe-run on recent data and compare with how UK lenders work today
Oversampled bad loans30% bad against a reported 5% in realityCosts and default chances are inflated and approval rates too low, so the real gap may be smallerReweight to a realistic default rate before setting thresholds
Only granted loansRejected applicants never appear, so any past bias in who got a loan is baked into the labelsReject inference methods, or data that includes rejected applicants
Broken sex variableOne code mixes women who are not single with single men, so sex or intersectional bias can't be testedUse datasets that record sex reliably
Missing informationNo comprehensive income and expenditure data, which a modern lender would useNot addressed in this project
Fix cut-off chosen out of fold, not in a full inner loopA little optimism could remain in the fix's resultsChoose the cut-off with an inner cross validation inside each training fold
No real recession in the dataTail risk numbers rely on an assumed correlationCalibrate to historical default rates across economic cycles
One definition of fairnessEqual approval rates and equal chances for good risks can't always both hold, and approval rate parity alone doesn't establish that a decision is fairReport several measures and explain the trade-off
Model riskResults depend on the chosen model, cost assumptions, validation approach and threshold selectionTreat the findings as an investigation, not evidence for deployment
